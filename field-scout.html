Zoom to your ground and tap your field, or trace the exact boundary. You get its soil, 10-year crop history, weather, drought, satellite crop-vigor and nearby cash bids, no account, no charge.

New here? Load a real Iowa field and see the full read, no drawing needed.

How the field math works

Every number in the read comes from one of these rules. Open one to check it by hand.

Acres

Worked from the corners you draw, on the WGS84 ellipsoid the GPS in your tractor uses (each latitude goes to its equal-area latitude, then the corners are summed). Check: a square one mile on a side at 41.5° N reads 640.00 acres, a quarter-quarter 40.00, a long eighty 80.00. Your own deed or FSA map can differ by the waterways and road ditches you left in or out.

Soil mix, CSR2 and productivity (NCCPI)

The USDA soil survey (SSURGO) returns every soil map unit inside your outline with its acres. Each share is that unit’s acres over the field’s. CSR2 (Iowa only) and NCCPI are acre-weighted averages of the units that carry a rating.

Worked example: 80 acres at CSR2 85, 60 acres at 70 and 20 acres unrated. (85 × 80 + 70 × 60) ÷ 140 = 78.6. The unrated 20 acres are left out, not counted as zero, and no field CSR2 is shown unless rated acres cover at least half the field.

Prime farmland is USDA’s legal farmland class for each map unit, not the capability class. Slope is the survey’s representative slope for each unit, acre-weighted.

Ten years of crops

The USDA Cropland Data Layer crop at the center point of your field, for each of the last ten published years, plus a check at up to four more points inside your outline (fields of 8 acres or more) that says in how many years they agree. CDL is satellite-classified at roughly 85–90% per pixel, so one odd year is more often a miss than a real change. A rotation is named only from the last four to six years. Corn on corn is flagged only when this year’s corn follows last year’s, or after three straight corn years, so a corn–corn–soybean farm is not told to break its rotation.

Rain and heat against past years

Open-Meteo’s ERA5 archive at the field center, January 1 through the latest archived day (about six days back), against the same calendar window in each of the five years before. That five-year average is what the page calls average; it is not a 30-year normal. Growing degree units use the corn method: daily high capped at 86°F, low raised to 50°F, base 50°F.

Hail, drought and vigor

Hail: National Weather Service storm reports of hail within 40 miles of the field center over this year and the four before. Reports follow people, so towns and highways count more, and a report nearby is not proof your field was hit. The radar swath check is NOAA’s estimated footprint against your drawn edge.

Drought: the US Drought Monitor class at the field center. If the service does not answer, the page says so; a failed read is never shown as “None”.

Vigor: Sentinel-2 NDVI averaged over your outline on each clear pass, set against this field’s own average for the same date in past years.

Cash bids, rent and county numbers

Bids are the nearest elevators posting to AGSIST’s bid network within 75 miles of the field’s ZIP, with the posting date. Rent is the USDA NASS county cash-rent survey; tenure is the Census of Agriculture; foreign-held acres are USDA AFIDA filings; the county record is the Farmland Atlas sheet. Each card names its source and year, and a year USDA did not publish stays blank.

Field Scout does not know who owns or farms the ground, what it yielded, or what went on it. Questions or a number that looks wrong: sig@farmers1st.com.

Where the data comes from

Field Scout reads public datasets when you draw a field. Each read is only as good as its source:

  • Soil types, capability class & slope: USDA NRCS SSURGO soil survey, queried for every map unit inside your field.
  • Soil productivity (NCCPI): USDA's National Commodity Crop Productivity Index, a national 0–1 scale derived from SSURGO.
  • 10-year crop rotation: USDA NASS Cropland Data Layer, satellite-classified at roughly 85–90% per-pixel accuracy.
  • Weather & growing-degree units: Open-Meteo, including the ERA5 reanalysis archive for season-vs-normal comparisons.
  • Drought status: the US Drought Monitor, the weekly D0–D4 drought scale.
  • Hail history: severe-storm reports via the Iowa Environmental Mesonet, sourced from NOAA.
  • Cash grain bids: AGSIST's own cash-bid feed, showing the nearest posting elevators.
  • Crop vigor map layer (NDVI): Sentinel-2 / Copernicus imagery, cloud-masked and averaged over your field for each pass.
  • Moisture map layer: Sentinel-1 radar backscatter, a soil-wetness proxy independent of cloud cover.
  • Nitrogen & canopy-moisture trend (NDRE, NDMI): Sentinel-2 red-edge and shortwave-infrared bands, cloud-masked and tracked pass to pass. These feed the numeric trend read in "The Read," not a separate map layer.
  • State crop condition: USDA NASS Crop Progress, the weekly good-to-excellent rating for your state, set against past seasons.
  • Owned vs rented ground: USDA Census of Agriculture land tenure for your county.
  • Foreign-held acres: USDA AFIDA filings (Agricultural Foreign Investment Disclosure Act), county totals.
  • County land record: the AGSIST Farmland Atlas county sheet: land value from the Census of Agriculture, the county's corn yield record and crop-insurance claims.
  • Parcel lines and owner of record: free public government parcel services only, read for the field you pick. Wisconsin: the Wisconsin Statewide Parcel Map. Other states as each county or state service is checked. Where there is none, the card says where to look.
  • Recorded transfers and sold land: Illinois, from the Illinois Department of Revenue MyDec transfer declarations (PTAX-203), and Wisconsin, from the Wisconsin Department of Revenue's monthly electronic transfer return files (the last five years, farm and forest land only). Other states are added once their free public data is read and checked.
  • Flood zones, wetlands, terrain: FEMA National Flood Hazard Layer, USFWS National Wetlands Inventory, USGS 3DEP.

Field Scout reads public survey and forecast data. It can't see what's actually happening in your field, your inputs, or your agronomist's plan. It's decision support, not a substitute for boots on the ground.

Field Scout: common questions

How do I find out what soil type my field is?

Drop a pin on your field (or trace its boundary), and Field Scout queries the USDA SSURGO soil survey for every soil map unit inside the polygon. You get each soil series by name (Clarion loam, Webster clay loam, and so on) along with its land capability class and slope, so you can see exactly how the ground varies across the field.

Where can I see what crops were grown on a field in past years?

Field Scout reads the USDA Cropland Data Layer (CDL), a satellite-classified record of what grew on each parcel each season, and shows the crop at the center of your field each year (USDA Cropland Data Layer) for the last ten available years. It's a quick way to check a field's rotation: useful for scouting, due diligence on rented or purchased ground, or spotting a corn-on-corn streak.

What is a soil's land capability class?

Land capability class is a USDA rating from 1 to 8 that describes how suited a soil is to row-crop production. Class 1 has few limitations; class 2–4 ground faces progressively more (slope, wetness, erosion risk); classes 5–8 are better suited to pasture, range, or woodland. Field Scout shows the non-irrigated capability class for each soil on your field so you can see where your strongest and weakest acres are.

What is NCCPI, the soil productivity index?

The National Commodity Crop Productivity Index (NCCPI) is a USDA index from 0 to 1 that rates inherent soil productivity for commodity crops on a single national scale. Because it's national, strong ground in a dry or sandy region can still read lower than Corn Belt mollisols, so Field Scout presents it as context, never as a verdict on your management.

How accurate is the crop history?

The Cropland Data Layer is satellite-classified at roughly 85–90% per-pixel accuracy. Field Scout names each year's crop from the pixel at your field's center point, and on fields of 8 acres or more it also reads up to four more points inside your outline (five in all) and says in how many years they all agree, so a split field or a waterway shows up. A single odd year in the sequence is more often a classification miss than a real planting change, so read the ten-year pattern rather than any one season.

Is corn-on-corn bad for my field?

Continuous corn isn't automatically wrong, but it carries a measurable yield drag, often 10 to 30 bushels an acre versus rotated corn in university trials, plus rising rootworm and disease pressure, and it forgoes the nitrogen a soybean year credits back. University trials find the drag is usually largest in dry, low-yield years and on weaker ground. Field Scout flags a streak and, where a cash bid is nearby, estimates what the drag is worth per acre.

How do I check the drought status of my field?

Field Scout pulls the current US Drought Monitor classification (None through D4, Exceptional Drought) for your field's location, so you can see at a glance whether moisture is an acute concern this week. It's the same scale USDA uses for disaster and program eligibility.

Can I see hail history for my area?

Yes. Turn on the Hail history layer and Field Scout maps severe-hail storm reports near your field over the last five years, including the largest stone sizes reported. It's a useful gut-check for crop-hail coverage decisions, with a direct link to the national hail map for the wider picture.

Where do the nearby cash grain bids come from?

Field Scout shows the closest cash corn and soybean bids to your field from AGSIST's own cash-bid feed, with the posting elevator named. It's there so you can see what the ground grows and what that crop brings at the nearest elevator today.

Does Field Scout work anywhere in the US?

Field Scout is national. Soil and crop-history coverage follow the underlying USDA datasets: SSURGO soil survey and the Cropland Data Layer, which blanket the cropland regions of the country. A few areas in the mountain West and parts of Alaska have soil-survey gaps; if a field falls in one, the soil panel says so.

Is Field Scout free? Do I need an account?

Field Scout is available at no charge, with no login and no account. It runs on free public data from the USDA, Copernicus Sentinel-2, Open-Meteo, and the US Drought Monitor, alongside AGSIST's own cash-bid feed. I built it for working US producers to use as often as they like.

Is my field data private?

Fields you save are stored only in your own browser on the device you're using, not on any server. Clearing your browser data removes them. Field Scout doesn't require an account and isn't tracking which fields you look at against your name. Questions? Reach me at sig@farmers1st.com.

What is a good NDVI value for corn or soybeans?

NDVI runs from about 0 (bare soil) toward 1 (dense green canopy). For corn and soybeans, readings climb through the vegetative stages and sit near the top of the range at full canopy. No single cutoff fits every crop, stage and region, so the better comparison is the field against its own multi-year normal for the same date, which Field Scout computes per field, because crop, stage, and region all move the number.

What does NDRE tell you about nitrogen?

NDRE is a red-edge index tied to chlorophyll. Its value is timing: red-edge light keeps discriminating after NDVI saturates over a closed canopy, so a fading NDRE while NDVI holds is the satellite pattern of chlorophyll thinning, often a nitrogen shortfall. It points, it doesn't diagnose: a tissue or soil nitrate test confirms it either way. Field Scout raises the flag while there's still time to sidedress.

Can I use Field Scout to evaluate land to rent or buy?

Yes. That's one of its best uses. Drawing a parcel shows you its soil classes and productivity index, average slope, ten years of what actually grew there from the satellite record (a quick rotation check), drought and hail history, and the nearest cash bids. The printable field report puts it all on one page for the file. It's public survey data, so treat it as due-diligence context alongside a walk of the ground, not an appraisal.
