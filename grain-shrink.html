Grain shrink: what wet grain nets in town, and what dry grain gives away

Sigurd Lindquist, CCA, Chetek, Wisconsin

How your elevator shrinks and charges
Up to moisture, %¢ a wet bushel

The numbers are on your elevator's discount sheet or the back of the scale ticket. Test weight for bushels: 56 lb.

Sell wet, or dry it myself?

Your own dryer takes the grain to the base above, then it sells at the same bid. This side counts fuel, electricity and the extra haul. It leaves out labor, dryer payments and repairs.

Gallons: Iowa State puts a high-temperature dryer at 0.018 gallon of LP a bushel a point on average, anywhere from 0.010 to 0.025 by dryer and weather. Purdue's column-dryer energy table works out to about 0.017 to 0.020 (corn from 28% or 22% down to 15%, at 92,000 BTU a gallon). Your own LP bill over last year's bushels and points is the best number.

Electricity is Purdue's formula: hp x 0.746 x price a kWh / dry bushels an hour. Their example, 42.5 hp at 300 bushels an hour and 7 cents, is 0.7 cents a bushel. Leave these blank and power is not counted.

All in: Iowa State figured 3 cents a bushel a point in 2019 ($1.00 LP, 14 cents power) and 4 cents in 2022 ($1.50 LP) with its A2-32 spreadsheet. Put in your own.

Iowa State used 1 cent a bushel a mile for the haul to the farm dryer, and 1% handling loss (0.22 to 1.71% measured on farms), taken off the wet weight.

How the elevator figures it

The buyer pays for dry bushels at a base moisture. Grain wetter than base gets shrunk on paper to what it would weigh dried to base, and most elevators also take a drying charge per wet bushel. Grain drier than base gets no credit: it is weighed at the standard test weight, so the water you dried off is grain you did not get paid for.

Water shrink is the weight of water taken out, as a share of the wet weight:

water shrink % = 100 x (wet moisture - base) / (100 - base) corn, 25% to 15.5%: 100 x 9.5 / 84.5 = 11.24%

Buyers add a little for dry matter lost in handling. Some use a drying table (water shrink plus about 0.5%); others use a flat factor per point, typically 1.2 to 1.5% (NCH-61). A flat factor carries more handling loss the wetter the corn. Iowa State measured 0.22 to 1.71% handling loss on farms and 0.64 to 1.33% at commercial dryers; NCH-61 calls more than 1% a loss greater than those surveys found. The calculator tells you what handling loss your elevator's factor assumes.

Overdry grain: what it gives away

The dry matter in the grain does not change when it dries. Only the water does. Delivered under base, you are paid on the weight you hauled; at base, the same grain would have weighed more and made more bushels:

bushels at base = bushels delivered x (100 - delivered moisture) / (100 - base) soybeans at 9%, 1,000 bu: 1,000 x 91 / 87 = 1,046 bu, 46 more

Questions

How is grain shrink calculated?

Water shrink is 100 times the points removed, divided by 100 minus the base moisture. Drying corn from 25% to 15.5% takes out 9.5 points: 9.5 x 100 / 84.5 = 11.24% of the wet weight. Buyers then add handling loss, either a flat 0.5% on a drying table (11.74% total) or by using a flat factor per point, such as 1.3% a point (12.35% total). That is from NCH-61, the National Corn Handbook shrink publication.

What is a normal shrink factor for corn?

NCH-61 says flat factors typically run 1.2 to 1.5% a point. Water alone is 1.183% a point when the base is 15.5%, so anything over that is handling loss. On 28% corn, a 1.25 factor assumes 0.83% handling loss and a 1.4 factor assumes 2.71%. Iowa State measured 0.22 to 1.71% on farms and 0.64 to 1.33% at commercial dryers; NCH-61 calls more than 1% a bigger loss than those surveys found.

Should I sell wet corn or dry it myself?

Run both. Iowa State's 2019 example: 1,000 wet bushels of 20.5% corn at $3.50, with a 4.8 cent drying charge a point and a 1.4% factor from a 15% base, nets $2,967 sold wet. Dried on the farm at their figured 3 cents a point, with 1% handling loss and $40 of extra hauling, the same corn netted $3,033. Your elevator's numbers and your own drying cost decide it: the "Sell wet, or dry it myself?" box on this page runs both sides with your LP, power and haul.

How much do overdry soybeans cost?

Beans delivered under 13% are paid on the weight you haul, so the water you dried off is beans you did not sell. At 9%, 1,000 delivered bushels would have been 1046 bushels at 13%, 46 more. Nebraska CropWatch's example: 75 bushels an acre at 13% harvested at 9% sells 3.3 fewer bushels an acre, $33 an acre at $10 beans.

What is the base moisture for corn, soybeans, wheat and sorghum?

Arkansas Extension's FSA1078 lists corn 15.5%, soybeans 13%, wheat 13.5% and grain sorghum 14%. Those are the defaults here. Your elevator's discount sheet is the one that counts, so change the base box to match it.

Is a moisture discount the same as shrink?

Not quite. Some buyers knock a share off the price instead of the bushels: Iowa State's example takes 2% of the price per point over 15%, 11% for 20.5% corn. Paid on wet bushels, that comes to the same dollars as a flat 2% a point shrink with no drying charge, so enter it that way.

Where does the bid come from?

Type your own, or enter your ZIP and the page uses the nearest elevator's posted bid for that grain from the same boards as the cash bids page. If that board was posted before today and states its futures month, the page re-marks it to today's futures the way the cash bids page does and shows the posted price beside it. With no ZIP, pick a state and the page uses the latest futures price less that state's median basis: the settle once it is recorded, otherwise the delayed quote with the time it was read. Sorghum has no state basis yet, so it needs your ZIP or your own bid.
