The 2026 Farm Bill

Two chambers, two bills: the House-passed H.R. 7567 and the Senate Agriculture Committee's Agricultural Act of 2026 (Farm Bill 2.0)

A plain, sourced look at where farm bill money actually goes, and what these bills actually change. Those are two different questions. The programs carry a $1.374 trillion ten-year baseline; each bill's own effect on that baseline is close to budget-neutral. Both numbers are true, and keeping them straight is the whole point.

What actually happened October 1

The extension ran out Sep 30 and was not renewed. Here is what that does, and does not, do to you.

Every story about this deadline says the farm bill “expired.” Almost nothing a working producer depends on expired with it, and the date that should worry you is not this one. The current extension (P.L. 119–37, Div. E §5002, enacted November 2025) ran through FY2026 and crop year 2026. Sep 30 ended the fiscal year. Crop-year programs run past it. The stopgap funding law that runs to December 11 did not extend the farm bill.

Did not change on October 1

  • Crop insurance. It runs on permanent statutory authority. It does not lapse, it has never lapsed, and no farm bill deadline touches your policy, your premium support or your guarantee.
  • Reference prices, ARC and PLC, base acres. Reauthorized by the 2025 reconciliation law (P.L. 119–21), reference prices raised, programs carried through 2031.
  • SNAP. Continues as long as Congress appropriates for it, which is a separate fight on a separate calendar.

Actually stopped

  • The programs reconciliation left without a budget baseline: nothing to spend, so nothing to continue. Named by CRS: Biobased Markets, Specialty Crop Block Grants, the Local Agriculture Market Program, Organic Agriculture Research and Extension, Animal Disease Prevention and Management, and Farming Opportunities Training and Outreach.
  • New CRP enrollment. FSA set Sep 30 as the last day to approve CRP contracts; after it the agency has no statutory authority to approve new ones, even with money left. FSA says a new notice will follow if Congress re-authorizes CRP (DTN, Oct 1, 2026).
  • They resume when a farm bill or another extension restores them. If you are not in one of those programs and not signing up new CRP acres, October 1 was a headline and not an event.

The date that actually matters is January 1, 2027

The suspension of permanent law (the 1938 and 1949 Acts that still sit underneath all of this) runs through the 2026 crop year. The 2025 reconciliation law did not extend it. CRS is blunt about why: those extensions “have not scored as having a budgetary effect,” so there was no reason to carry one in a reconciliation bill.

Without congressional action, permanent law begins to take effect January 1, 2027. Dairy goes first, then wheat on June 1 and corn on October 1. In July 2025 the permanent-law parity support price for milk was 252% of the market price of milk: USDA would be required to buy milk until the market got there.

That is the cliff. It is three months past the deadline everyone was writing about. Both bills would fix it by extending the suspension through crop year 2031, but neither is law, and the Senate committee's September 16 vote does not change that. The bill still needs a Senate floor vote and a conference with the House, and a short-term extension in the meantime would not fix it either unless the extension carries the suspension with it.

Sources: Congressional Research Service, R48775: The Farm Bill After FY2025 Budget Reconciliation, for the permanent-law suspension expiring with crop year 2026, the January 1, 2027 reversion and its commodity order, the 252% July 2025 parity figure, the unfunded program list, and the citation for the current extension. We have not independently confirmed the parity figure against USDA’s own tables. It is CRS’s number and is labelled as such. If a September extension changes any of this, it changes here the same week.

05 Where it stands

The path to the President's desk

Both chambers now have a bill. The House passed H.R. 7567 in April; the Senate released its own draft on June 23 rather than take up the House bill directly, and its Agriculture Committee passed it on September 16. A Senate floor vote needing 60 votes and a conference to reconcile the two versions remain, and the September 30 extension has already lapsed.

What’s actually in the way · as of Oct 6

  • Committee is done. The floor is not. The Senate Agriculture Committee passed the Agricultural Act of 2026, 12–11, on September 16, after failing along party lines on August 6. Sen. Mitch McConnell returned from a months-long absence to cast the deciding vote. That clears committee. It does not clear the Senate.
  • The 60-vote math still applies. A farm bill needs 60 votes to pass the full Senate, and Republicans hold nowhere near that on their own. The committee vote was party-line; nothing about it shows the bill has the bipartisan support a floor vote requires. Reporting as of Sep 16 said a floor vote before Congress's pre-midterm recess looked unlikely.
  • Conference is still ahead. Even a Senate floor win only produces a Senate bill. It would still need to be reconciled with the House-passed H.R. 7567 in conference before either version reaches the President, and that has not started.

One for the insurance industry, not your policy: the Senate bill also writes the 2025 law’s higher administrative-and-operating reimbursement rates for insurance providers into statute for 2026 and later reinsurance years. Your revenue guarantee math doesn’t change.

06 Questions, answered

Farm bill FAQ

Short, sourced answers to the questions people actually search, the same answers carried in this page's structured data.

What is the 2026 Farm Bill?
The 2026 Farm Bill is an omnibus bill that would reauthorize USDA farm, food, conservation, and rural programs for five years, through fiscal year 2031. Two versions now exist: the House passed H.R. 7567 (the Farm, Food, and National Security Act of 2026) on April 30, 2026, and the Senate Agriculture Committee passed its own version, the Agricultural Act of 2026 (branded Farm Bill 2.0), on September 16, 2026.
Is the 2026 Farm Bill law?
No. The House passed its version in April, and the Senate Agriculture Committee passed its version 12-11 on September 16, but neither has been enacted. The Senate bill still needs a full Senate floor vote, and the two chambers must reconcile their bills before either reaches the President. The extension of the 2018 farm bill expired September 30, 2026, and the stopgap funding law through December 11 did not renew it.
How much does the 2026 Farm Bill cost?
Programs carry a roughly $1.374 trillion ten-year baseline (FY2027–FY2036) under current law. Each bill's own effect on that baseline is small: the House bill scores close to budget-neutral, and the Senate draft is also described as budget-neutral but is not yet scored in detail.
Where does most farm bill money go?
Nutrition, mainly SNAP, is about 71.7% of the baseline. Four titles account for roughly 99% of the money: Nutrition, Crop Insurance, Commodities, and Conservation.
What does the Senate's Agricultural Act of 2026 change?
It modernizes USDA farm loan limits (guaranteed operating loans to $3 million, ownership to $3.5 million), raises conservation easement cost-share and adds new easement and state conservation programs, more than doubles Market Access Program and Foreign Market Development trade funding, expands crop insurance research for specialty crops, and writes rural broadband into law. The version the committee passed also allows year-round E15 sales and delays the SNAP state cost-share by a year. It builds on the 2025 reconciliation law and is described as budget-neutral.
When did the current farm bill expire?
The extension of the 2018 farm bill expired September 30, 2026, and Congress has not passed another. The stopgap funding law through December 11 keeps the government funded but does not extend the farm bill. New CRP enrollment is paused, and without action permanent law begins to take effect January 1, 2027.
What happened when the farm bill lapsed on September 30, 2026?
Less than the headlines suggested, and the real deadline is later. Crop insurance runs on permanent statutory authority and does not lapse. Reference prices, ARC/PLC and base acres were reauthorized by the 2025 reconciliation law (P.L. 119–21) through 2031. SNAP continues as long as Congress appropriates for it. What actually stops are the programs reconciliation left without a budget baseline: Biobased Markets, Specialty Crop Block Grants, the Local Agriculture Market Program, Organic Agriculture Research and Extension, Animal Disease Prevention and Management, and Farming Opportunities Training and Outreach, plus new CRP enrollment, which FSA cannot approve without authority, and they resume when a bill or extension funds them. The date that matters more is January 1, 2027: the suspension of permanent law runs only through the 2026 crop year, reconciliation did not extend it, and without action the 1938 and 1949 Acts begin to take effect: dairy first, then wheat on June 1 and corn on October 1. Source: CRS R48775.
What's the difference between the House and Senate bills?
Both use the same twelve-title structure and both build on the 2025 reconciliation law. The clearest differences so far: the House bill cuts EQIP by $786 million and redistributes it, while the Senate draft emphasizes raising loan limits, conservation easement cost-share, and trade promotion. The House version has passed and been scored; the Senate Agriculture Committee passed its version 12-11 along party lines on September 16, after failing on the same lines August 6, and it has not yet been scored or taken up by the full Senate.
What is holding the farm bill up right now?
The Senate Agriculture Committee passed the Agricultural Act of 2026 12-11 on September 16, with Sen. Mitch McConnell returning to cast the deciding Republican vote after a months-long absence. That clears the committee, but the bill still needs 60 votes on the full Senate floor: a threshold Republicans cannot reach alone, and then a conference with the House to reconcile the two bills. As of September 16, reporting said a Senate floor vote before Congress’s pre-midterm recess looked unlikely, and final action has now slipped past the September 30 lapse of the extension.
