Urea Loss in Dry Soil Conditions

In dry soil conditions with a urease inhibitor, volatilization losses are generally very low because the volatilization reaction itself requires moisture to get started.

June 4, 2026

- In dry soil conditions with a urease inhibitor, volatilization losses are generally very low because the volatilization reaction itself requires moisture to get started.

- If it stays dry for weeks: Urea is fine sitting there. It won't volatilize without moisture.

- If you get a light shower (< 0.25"): The NBPT clock starts. You've got ~7–14 days before significant losses begin.

- If you get ≥ 0.5" rain: Urea moves into the soil and volatilization risk drops to near zero.

Should we apply urea without rain in the forecast?

Yes, because urea volatilization requires moisture.

The process works like this:

- Urea dissolves in soil moisture

- Urease enzyme (in soil organic matter and crop residue) hydrolyzes urea

- Then ammonium carbonate breaks down

- NH₃ gas escapes to the atmosphere

If the soil surface is truly dry, Step 1 doesn't happen efficiently — the urea granule just sits there intact. No hydrolysis = no ammonia gas = no volatilization.

When to be concerned about urea loss

A light rain or dew dissolves the urea just enough to start hydrolysis but not enough rain to move it into the soil (need ≥ 0.25–0.5" to incorporate), then warm, windy conditions drive off the ammonia gas. That moist-then-drying cycle on warm days is where you see the big losses — up to 30–40% of applied N without an inhibitor.

Note on UAN

50% of the nitrogen in UAN is in the urea form and dissolved in liquid, so there is a risk of volatilization. Mitigation strategies are injection into soil and a NBPT-based urease inhibitor.
