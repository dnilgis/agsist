Questions

What do the layers show?

Staircase steps up with every rain, so a flat stretch is a dry stretch and a tall step is a big rain. Roots draws each day as its own line, snow stacked above the ground and rain soaking down below it. Usual year draws a usual year beside yours: NOAA’s 30-year normal, or this station’s own average over every year it has kept. Month normals puts that usual month total in gray under each month. Remarkable days marks the biggest snow, the wettest week, and any month or year that stands out in that station’s own history, if it has kept records long enough to tell. Frost dates puts a small mark on the date line for the last frost in spring and the first in fall. Hottest, coldest names those two days in the small print; with Warmer or colder each day on, they are named on the stripes instead. Warmer or colder each day adds a strip of stripes under the chart, one a day, blue colder and rust warmer than the station’s normal for that date. Crop places corn or soybean stages between the planting and harvest days you set, Plants draws them as little plants, and Usual-year plants adds the same crop on a usual year’s temperatures in gray. Every year behind yours draws every full year the station kept as thin gray lines behind yours. More on each below.

Where does the rain data come from?

From the nearest weather station with a good record for that year, from NOAA’s daily records. Some are National Weather Service stations, some are rain gauges kept by volunteers; the line under the picture says which. The print names the station and how far it is from town, in a small line at the bottom. It’s that station’s gauge, so a storm that hit the farm and missed the station won’t show. If there’s a station closer to the farm, pick it under Weather station, under the town box.

What does “a usual year” mean?

When you compare with NOAA’s 30-year normal, a usual year is that normal for the station. When you compare with the station’s full record, it’s the station’s own average over every year it has kept. Either way, a day the station didn’t report is left out of both totals. The print says which one it used.

What if the station missed days?

Missing days show as gaps, never as dry days, and a small line on the print says what a gap is. The page reads the three nearest stations and picks the first with 10 or fewer missing days. A station missing 11 to 31 days can still be drawn, and the line under the print says how many. A station missing more than 31 days in the year gets no print, because the gaps would look like dry weeks that may not have been dry. A morning reading is filed under the day it was read, so an evening storm can show on the next day.

Does it show snow?

Yes, where the station kept a snowfall record nearly every day. Snow is drawn above the line, never below it, and looks different from rain, so you can tell a snow day from a rain at a glance. If the station didn’t keep snowfall, the snow is still counted, melted down to water the way the gauge measures it, but it can’t be drawn as snow. The year in numbers, under Your words, says which.

How are the crop stages placed?

Pick corn or soybeans under Layers, then set the planting day and the harvest day right there. Each stage goes on the day the growing degree days at the station reached it, counted from planting the same way as the GDU calculator: each day’s high and low held between 50°F and 86°F, averaged, less 50. Corn stages use Purdue’s NCH-40 numbers for a 2,700 GDU hybrid. Soybeans show emergence and V3 only: their flowering and maturity depend on day length and variety, so they are not drawn. A day with no temperature reading stops the count, and no later stage is claimed. Compare with a usual year runs the same crop on NOAA’s 1991 to 2020 normal temperatures and says how many days ahead or behind silking (corn) or V3 (soybeans) came.

Where do the frost dates and temperatures come from?

From the nearest weather station that kept daily highs and lows that year, often the same one as the rain; the print names it. Frost is a low of 32°F or colder: the last one before August and the first one after July get a small mark on the date line. If a missing reading could hide a later or earlier frost, that frost is left off. The hottest and coldest day go in the small print. Daily temperatures adds a strip of stripes under the chart, one for each day: blue when the day’s average was colder than the station’s 1991 to 2020 normal for that date, rust when warmer, darker the farther off; the hottest and coldest days are named on it. A day with no reading is left blank. A station without NOAA normals gets no stripes, and the print says so.

What are the gray lines behind the year?

That’s the Every year behind your year option. Each gray line is another full year the station kept, every day reported, drawn the same way as yours, with your year in blue on top. The two wettest and two driest of them have their year written at the end of the line, and the print says where your year ranks. It needs at least 30 full years, so the print uses the nearest station with that long a record, which may be farther away; the page says how far and why, and the print names it. Staircase only.

Yes. Drawing it is free and so are the numbers. Once your year is drawn, open The year in numbers, under Your words. It shows the year’s rain and melted snow, the snowfall, the wettest day, the longest dry stretch and, if you pick something to compare with, a usual year’s total. You only pay if you want the print. Can I make one for our wedding year or the year a child was born?

Yes. Pick the year, anywhere back to 1890 if the station kept records that long, and mark the day itself under Mark a day. You can mark up to 5 days, so the wedding, a birthday and the year’s first harvest can all go on one print. Put the names on the name line and a few words on the line under it.
