## Bugs
- **Low: two answers for the same field.** With the Clayton defaults, the per-bushel scaler gives $266. The share-of-gross method rounds the share to 28.9% first and gives $265. The exact share (266 / 919.2 = 28.94%) gives $265.80, which should display as $266.
- **Low: two names for one trend.** The scaler says "our 15-year fit", while the table note says "the county's 14-yr trend yield". It is the same number: n = 14 points in a 15-year window.
- **Low, framing: "held flat" while the USDA average fell.** The Illinois hero says "RENT HELD FLAT, +0.0% vs 2025". Over the same year, NASS's own Illinois average fell from $264 to $261 (farmdoc, Sep 15). The median of matched counties is correctly computed, but a reader who saw farmdoc will think we disagree.
- **Verify, medium honesty risk: the release cadence and date.**
  - The page promises "The 2027 rents usually come out in August 2027". The saved NASS guide copy says "The Cash Rents Survey is conducted every other year", and lists "October 6, 2026" as this year's Methodology and Quality Measures date.
  - Our own history has annual county data from 2019 to 2026, so the practice has been annual. But the promise and the "released Aug 21, 2026" date are not confirmed by any saved copy.
  - Confirm both at the NASS release before keeping them on 47 pages.

## Where we win
- **Every county in 47 states, 2008 to 2026, from one source.** The tables are sortable, with irrigated and pasture columns, YoY on matched counties, and a "what this can't tell you" note. Every Illinois figure I checked matches farmdoc's reading of the same release.
- **A number for your own ground.** The county's rent per trend bushel is scaled to your yield, labeled as derived, and says where it does not apply ("mixed" irrigated counties, ended series). No readable competitor gives a parcel-level starting point. UNL says its survey "should not be used to set a specific rental rate". ND says its prices "should never be used as the only factor".
- **Tied to the land record.** Each county links its Atlas page (rent share by year, yield, insurance), the state's lease-termination law and the printable lease. It also links ARC/PLC and PRF for the state.

## Where they beat us
- **The state average and the outlook for next year (farmdoc).** farmdoc opens with "The average cash rental rate reported by the USDA for cropland in Illinois was $261 per acre for 2026, a small decline from ... $264". It ends with a 2027 outlook: "further reductions also seem unlikely", citing ISPFMRA's -$3 to -$5. It also notes that the survey "expectations for 2027 were based on survey responses that were collected prior to the recent commodity price rally". We show neither the state average nor any 2027 view, during the months when 2027 rents are being set.
- **Rent by quality class (farmdoc citing ISPFMRA).** ISPFMRA gives "$227 per acre on fair quality farmland to $384 per acre on excellent". Our yield scaler is our own answer to this question, but no quality-class reference is linked from the Illinois page.
- **Spread and sample size inside the county (ND Land Dept).** For each county ND prints the minimum, maximum and most frequent reported rent, the number of reports, a 2021-2025 five-year average, and the price of rented land. It also warns readers to "exercise discretion when using data from counties with less than fifteen reports". We give one mean per county, with no sense of spread or reliability.
- **Rent by land type, and pasture by the pair (UNL).** UNL gives center-pivot and gravity irrigated rents separately, dryland and pasture by region, and "Cow-Calf Pairs $/Month" ($69 to $82). It puts land values next to them, which is the input to the return-on-value method we leave blank.

## Five changes, ranked
1. **Show the NASS state average next to the median and reconcile the headline (S).**
   - What: put the state average first, with its YoY. Keep the median-of-matched-counties line and say why they differ. When the two disagree in direction, word the hero so it does not read "held flat" against a USDA decline. On Iowa, name ISU's $270 and why it differs. Use one small state-average file.
   - Honesty risk: low.
2. **A dated "what 2027 may do" line per state (S/M).**
   - What: where a university or society publishes an outlook (farmdoc/ISPFMRA for IL, ISU, UNL), cite it with its date. Elsewhere, compute the 2026 rent as a share of gross at Dec '27 futures less a typical basis, against the same share at 2026 prices, and say which way that points.
   - Honesty risk: medium. It must be labeled "derived, not a forecast", and the derived line must never be presented as a predicted rent.
3. **Confirm and fix the release cadence and date (S).** Check the NASS release history. Replace "usually come out in August 2027" with what NASS has actually announced, and correct "Aug 21" if needed. This fixes the verify item. Honesty risk: low. Leaving it unchecked is the risk.
4. **A noise signal per county from our own history (S).**
   - What: show the county's 2022-2026 average, and flag counties whose rent swings more than X% a year. For example: "this county's survey mean has moved ±$25 a year; treat one year with care". The data is already in the state JSON, so there is no new page weight.
   - Honesty risk: low.
5. **A current price option in share of gross (S).** Next to the NASS 2025 average price ($4.10 in Iowa), offer "Dec '27 futures less basis" for the year being negotiated. Round the share and the dollars from the exact value so both methods agree on the same ground. This fixes the low bug. Honesty risk: low.

## Verdict
**Yes for "cash rent in <county>, any state".** No one else gives every county in 47 states, current, matched to farmdoc's figures, with a derived number for your own ground. farmdoc still answers "what is the state average, and where is 2027 going" better, and ND shows the spread inside a county that a single mean hides.

**Change since Oct 15:** 44 to 47 (+3). Local went from 4 to 5 with the your-ground scaler, depth from 4 to 5 with the fair-rent methods, and unique from 4 to 5. The Oct 15 competitor cells were snippet-based and are not comparable one to one.
