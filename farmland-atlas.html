      July nights: 2029 of 3135 counties are warming since 1976 with the 95% interval clear of zero, 0 cooling, 1106 with no clear direction; the median trend is +0.45 F per decade. In 2178 of 3140 counties the 2010s were the warmest complete decade since the 1900s (the 2020s are not complete and are not counted).

      Each county has at most 51 Julys since 1976, and a single county's trend has to be large to clear its own 95% interval. Fitted on every July since 1895 instead, 2614 of 3135 counties are warming with the interval clear of zero.

      Water: 608 of 2161 counties irrigate a quarter or more of their harvested cropland and 412 irrigate half or more; the median county irrigates 4%. In 886 of 2663 counties nine-tenths or more of the irrigation water is pumped from the ground (USGS 2015: crop irrigation where USGS splits it out, 1672 counties; all irrigation including golf courses in the other 991).

      Loss record: $210.7 billion of crop insurance indemnities in closed crop years since 1989 across 2840 counties (nominal dollars; the crop year still being paid is left out; pasture and rangeland rainfall-index policies excluded). Heat and drought share by period: 1989-1999 27% · 2000-2009 42% · 2010-2019 42% · 2020-2025 46%. Paid for failure of irrigation supply or equipment: $3.4 billion.

      Irrigated against dry cash rent: 475 counties publish both with enough paired years and enough dry rent to test (97 more are not tested: dry rent under $25). Measured as irrigated rent over dry rent, so general rent inflation cancels out, the multiple is rising in 77 and falling in 54, with the 95% interval clear of zero; by chance alone about 12 of 475 would be called each way. Falling fastest: Box Butte County, NE: 8.10 times dry rent in 2013 to 5.34 in 2026; Thurston County, WA: 6.12 times dry rent in 2009 to 2.20 in 2026; Lawrence County, AR: 3.31 times dry rent in 2012 to 1.67 in 2026.       What the Atlas is

      What this puts on one page for each county: how warm its July nights are and which way they are going; how much of its cropland only works with a pump and where that water comes from; what has destroyed crops there since 1989 and whether the insurance book has paid out more than it took in; what the census says the land is worth and what rent buys of that; how much sits in CRP and when those contracts end; how many weeks of each year since 2000 it spent in drought; how much solar and wind is on the ground and proposed; and, where a state publishes the register, its wells and water rights. Each is its own layer. I never add them together, because a combined score hides which problem the ground has.

      How it is measured

      Every number comes from a public federal or state file, named with its year. A county with nothing published shows a dash and the reason. The crop year still being settled is left out of every insurance figure and shown apart.

      Questions

      Why are heat and water graded separately instead of combined into one score?

      Because a combined score hides which problem a county has. A county with cool nights and a falling aquifer is a different purchase from a county with hot nights and rain-fed ground, and one number would call them the same.

      What does the July night temperature measure?

      The monthly mean of each day's minimum temperature for the county, in degrees Fahrenheit, from NOAA NCEI's nClimDiv county dataset, which runs from 1895. July is the silking month for corn across most of these states. Extension research puts the harm line for corn at nightly lows above 70 F, so a July whose average low was at or above 70 F is counted as a hot July.

      Where does the crop insurance loss record come from?

      USDA Risk Management Agency's Summary of Business cause-of-loss files, one per crop year since 1989. Each indemnity is grouped by the peril that caused it: heat and drought, excess moisture and flood, hail, wind, freeze and frost, irrigation failure, price decline, and other. Dollar totals and shares come from these files. The loss ratio comes from RMA's county Summary of Business instead, which carries premium on every policy; the cause-of-loss files carry premium only on policies that had a loss.

      How does irrigated rent compare with dry rent?

      Irrigated cropland cash rent divided by non-irrigated cash rent, same county, same year, from the USDA NASS Cash Rents Survey. A multiple rather than a dollar gap, because rents rose almost everywhere and a dollar gap widens even when nothing about the water changed. Irrigated rent also pays for the well, pump and pivot, so the multiple is not the price of water alone. Its direction is called only where the 95% interval of the fitted trend excludes zero and dry rent is at least $25 an acre.

      What is a crop insurance loss ratio and why does it matter for land?

      Indemnities paid divided by total premium, from USDA RMA's Summary of Business county files, every policy since 1989 except the rainfall and vegetation index pasture plans (PRF), which are counted separately. The open crop year is left out. At 1.00 the county's insurance book paid out what it took in; above it, claims exceeded the whole premium, the farmer's share and the federal subsidy together. Insurers, lenders and anyone pricing a lease look at it because it is the longest continuous record of how often crops in that county have failed against what was expected, with the dollars attached.

      Where does the land value come from, and is it an appraisal?

      The USDA Census of Agriculture, which asks every operator the market value of their land and buildings and publishes the average per acre by county for 2012, 2017 and 2022. It is not an appraisal and not a sale price; it is what operators reported, buildings included. This page shows it as published and divides the county's non-irrigated cash rent by it.

      Why are irrigation wells shown for some states and not others?

      Because only some states publish an open well register with an irrigation code that can be read and checked. The Atlas reads Nebraska's registered wells and Kansas's WIMAS water rights, and from September 2026 the registers of Minnesota, Michigan, Ohio, Wisconsin, Oklahoma, California, Arizona, Idaho, Oregon and Nevada, each where that month's pull succeeds. Registers differ in what they count and how far back they go, so compare a county with its own state. A county in any other state reads "no open register read yet".

      How much American farmland does China own?

      Not much: 247,659 acres at the end of 2024, about 0.5% of the foreign-held acres in USDA's AFIDA detail files (our sum of the rows; Hong Kong is listed on its own, 144,090 acres). Canada holds 16.1 million. Chinese-connected holdings are politically sensitive and several states have restricted them, but by acreage they are a small slice. Since 2023 the filings carry specific flags for secondary interests from China, Iran, Russia, and North Korea.

      Is 46 million acres a lot?

      USDA puts it at 3.6% of privately held agricultural land. In USDA’s detail files it has nearly doubled since 2010, from 24.8 million row-summed acres to 47.2 million, and that growth is the more important number. About 22 million of the 47 million acres are forest.

      Why is so much of it forest?

      Because global timber companies and investment funds own large tracts of U.S. timberland, and forest counts as agricultural land under AFIDA. Roughly 22 of the 47 million acres are forest, the single biggest reason raw AFIDA totals get misread as "foreign corn ground."

      My county shows nothing. Does that mean no foreign ownership?

      It means no disclosed holdings. AFIDA relies on self-reporting with civil penalties for failure to file; federal auditors have found compliance gaps. Absence of a filing is evidence, not proof.

      Where can I get the raw data?

      USDA FSA publishes the detailed holdings files annually, linked below. The Atlas adds them up without changing any figure.
