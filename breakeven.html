Break-even calculator: common questions

How do I calculate break-even price per bushel?
Divide your total cost per acre by your expected yield in bushels. For example: $850 per acre total cost ÷ 200 bu/ac expected yield = $4.25/bu break-even price for corn. This is the minimum cash price at which you cover all expenses. Because cash = futures + basis and basis is usually negative, the futures price you need is this number minus your basis (e.g. $4.25 cash at a -$0.30 basis means $4.55 futures). The calculator above does this as you type. To stay profitable, your market price (futures + basis) needs to exceed this number.
What is a realistic corn production cost per acre in Wisconsin or Minnesota?
farmdoc’s Illinois Crop Budgets for 2027 put corn costs other than land at $873 to $885 an acre, and $1,062 to $1,206 with their average cash rent of $186 to $321. Farms with lower yields usually spend less on fertilizer and drying. Land is the biggest variable: 2026 USDA NASS county cash rents for non-irrigated cropland reach about $247/acre in Wisconsin and $280/acre in Minnesota, with the median county at $136 in Wisconsin and $189 in Minnesota. See your county on AGSIST cash rent by state. Fertilizer and seed are the next biggest lines in those budgets. This calculator opens on farmdoc’s northern Illinois budget; pick your county to put in its rent and trend yield, then adjust each line to your own costs.
How does cash rent affect my corn or soybean break-even price?
For a tenant, rent is usually the biggest single cost per acre, and every dollar of it lands on the break-even. Each $50 an acre of rent adds about $0.28/bu to corn at 180 bu/ac, $0.25/bu at 200 bu/ac, and about $0.91/bu to soybeans at 55 bu/ac. It comes back every year, so the rent talk with your landlord matters.
What costs should I include in a full farm break-even calculation?
A complete break-even includes two layers. Direct inputs: seed, fertilizer (N, P, K, lime, sulfur), herbicides (pre and post-emerge), fungicide, insecticide, crop insurance premium (net of subsidy), drying, and trucking. Overhead costs: land rent or property tax/ownership cost, machinery depreciation, fuel and lube, repairs and maintenance, labor (including your own time at a market wage), operating loan interest, and general farm overhead. Many producers undercount depreciation and their own time: in farmdoc’s 2027 Illinois corn budgets machinery depreciation alone is $80 to $96 an acre, and those budgets leave out the operator’s labor, so what is left is the return to you and your land.
Where do the calculator’s default costs come from?
The corn and soybean lines open on farmdoc daily’s Illinois Crop Budgets for 2027 (Table 1, released August 2026, published September 1, 2026), northern Illinois unless you pick another region: their seed, pesticides, crop insurance, power costs as machinery, hired labor, the overhead items as overhead, their average cash rent and their trend yield. Fertilizer is not theirs: it is priced from co-op posted prices. Corn N is UW A2809’s rate for corn after soybeans at the posted N:corn price ratio (Table 6.1, the rate Fast Facts shows), the same at any yield. P and K are 0.38 lb P₂O₅ and 0.29 lb K₂O a bushel of corn (A2809 removal), so they move with your yield. Pick your county and the rent, the trend yield and an ARC/PLC estimate come from USDA NASS and AGSIST’s ARC/PLC figures, each labeled under its line. Wheat lines are this page’s own example, not a published budget.
What is the what-if sensitivity table and how do I use it?
The table shows profit or loss per acre for 36 pairs of yield and price. Rows are yields above and below yours, columns are prices above and below yours, and your own inputs are outlined in green. Red is a loss, green is a profit. It answers "what if yield comes up short and the price drops too?" in one look, which helps when you weigh insurance coverage levels or a forward sale.
How much profit per acre is corn expected to make in 2027?
Not much. farmdoc’s 2027 Illinois budgets project corn at $28 to $36 an acre over all costs, cash rent included, in northern and central Illinois, and a $61 an acre loss in southern Illinois. Soybeans look better: $68 to $86 north and central, $18 south. Those use $5 corn and $12 soybeans.
How much does a small move in corn price change my return per acre?
More than you’d think. farmdoc says a $0.50 move in corn price shifts Illinois returns by more than $100 an acre, because high yields multiply every cent. At 237 bushels, 50 cents is $118.50 an acre. The what-if table above shows it for your yield.
