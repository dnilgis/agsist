What this is. USDA’s weekly condition ratings are trained field observation, not yield measurement, useful because the method hasn’t changed in decades, and that consistency is what lets one year be ranked against another. This page ranks the ratings and stops there. For what they point to in bushels, beside USDA’s number and every other published forecast, see 2026 crop size: every forecast.
Questions people actually ask
Is this the worst crop we’ve ever had?

Check your state’s rank above. If it says 3 of 24, it’s genuinely historic. If it says 12 of 24, it’s a middling year that feels worse because it’s yours.

Why does my county look worse than my state’s number?

State ratings average enormous areas; your county, and your farm, can sit far from that average. The rank tells you how the state-level picture compares historically, not what your back forty looks like.

Do conditions predict yield?

Loosely and late. Correlation strengthens as the season ages. Early-summer G+E is a weak predictor; by dent stage it means much more. That relationship (with its R²) is on the Conditions vs Yield page.

Where do the numbers come from?

USDA NASS Crop Progress, published Monday afternoons in season. Each state is compared with every year USDA rated it at the same week; the query starts in 2000, and this fall no state has a 2000 rating at this week, so the records start in 2001 or later. Fetched and ranked automatically after each Monday release (Tuesday when a Monday holiday moves it); source linked below.

Source

USDA NASS Quick Stats, weekly crop condition (PCT GOOD + PCT EXCELLENT), state level, 2000–present. Rankings computed by scripts/fetch_conditions.py in the AGSIST repository. This year’s rating must come from the exact current week: a state USDA did not rate this week is left off, never shown with last week’s number; past years are matched to the same week ±1 so calendar drift does not drop a year. States with <10 comparable years omitted. Related: basis vs normal · drought monitor · yield estimator.
