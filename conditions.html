What this is. USDA’s weekly condition ratings are trained field observation, not yield measurement, useful because the method hasn’t changed in decades, and that consistency is what lets one year be ranked against another. The table ranks the ratings and stops there. For what they point to in bushels, beside USDA’s number and every other published forecast, see how big the crop is below.

What this can’t tell you. R² is explanation, not prediction, and a late-season rating "predicting" yield is partly just the season being over. Fits are against detrended yield (state yields drift up ~2 bu/ac/yr on genetics alone; without detrending, that drift fakes signal; the raw fit is kept in the data for anyone who wants it). Weeks with under 15 paired years are left blank rather than thinly fitted. And 26 years is still a small sample: treat differences of a few points as noise.

Questions people actually ask
Is this the worst crop we’ve ever had?

Check your state’s rank above. If it says 3 of 24, it’s genuinely historic. If it says 12 of 24, it’s a middling year that feels worse because it’s yours.

Why does my county look worse than my state’s number?

State ratings average enormous areas; your county, and your farm, can sit far from that average. The rank tells you how the state-level picture compares historically, not what your back forty looks like.

Do USDA crop condition ratings actually predict final yield?
Partially, late, and it depends where you farm. In week 40 (the last week with a fit; week 42 has under 15 paired years), the Good+Excellent share explains about 36% of how Iowa's final corn yield differs from its trend (22 years). Iowa's fit peaks at week 39, at about 64% (26 years). The two weeks rest on different years (22 vs 26), so the gap between them is partly a change of sample, not only of signal. In Tennessee, week 40's ratings explained 90% (22 years). This page computes the number for every state and week instead of asserting it.
Why do the ratings explain so little in early summer?
Because the yield isn't made yet. July corn in Iowa can rate 80% Good+Excellent and then hit August heat during grain fill. Ratings measure how the crop looks; yield is decided by what happens next. The R-squared rises through the season because each week more of the yield is already made.
What does the slope number mean?
It's the historical relationship: how many bushels per acre of yield deviation (from that state's long-term trend) has come with each 1-point move in Good+Excellent share, at that week. Illinois at week 39: about +0.86 bu/ac per G+E point.
Why detrend the yields?
State yields rise about two bushels per acre per year from genetics and agronomy alone. Without removing that trend, the fit confuses 'years got later' with 'ratings got better' and reports fake signal. We fit ratings against each year's deviation from trend; the raw fit is also kept in the data.
So should I trade off Monday's crop conditions number?
This page shows exactly how much that number has historically been worth in your state at this week, often less than the market reaction implies. When the market moves more than that, it is reacting to more than the rating. See what's already priced on the What's Priced In page.
Where do the numbers come from?

USDA NASS Crop Progress, published Monday afternoons in season. Each state is compared with every year USDA rated it at the same week; the query starts in 2000, and this fall no state has a 2000 rating at this week, so the records start in 2001 or later. Fetched and ranked automatically after each Monday release (Tuesday when a Monday holiday moves it); source linked below.

Source

USDA NASS Quick Stats, weekly crop condition (PCT GOOD + PCT EXCELLENT), state level, 2000–present. Ranked by AGSIST from that file.
