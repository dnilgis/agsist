Why basis is wide this fall: more grain than bins

8 of 11 states with full NASS figures have more grain to hold this fall than bins; Missouri is most crowded at 1.17×. The US as a whole: grain to hold is 99.6% of bin space (25,376 million bu, 25,489 of bins; NASS national figures).

Iowa: 1.08× 6th most crowded of 11

477 million bu in the bins on Sept 1 + 3,416 million bu of 2026 crop, against 3,590 million bu of bin space.

Corn basis in USDA’s Iowa region, week of Oct 9: −43¢, 14¢ under its 5-year normal for that week. Bins are one cause; freight and local demand are the others.

Bin space: 3,590 million bu on Dec 1, 2025, +0.6% from Dec 1, 2020 (NASS).

Past falls in Iowa (2000 to 2025, 26 with full figures): the September pressure averaged 0.99× and December 1 use 0.76×, so moving and using grain took off 24 points by December (most years 21 to 26). This fall’s 1.08× is above 23 of those 26. At the usual pace December 1 use would land near 0.85×; at the pace of the slowest tenth of years, 0.88×. Tried on past years, that usual-pace rule missed December by 2 points on average, 5 at worst.

Basis has to pay for space. When the grain that has to be held this fall is more than the bins can take, elevators bid less to slow it down or pay to ship it out, and that comes out of your basis. Grain to hold is September 1 stocks plus the 2026 corn, soybean and sorghum crop (latest NASS estimate); over 1.00× means more than the bins.

That does not mean the bins run out. farmdoc daily (Dhakal and Janzen, “Enough Room for the Harvest? Grain Storage Pressure in the Corn Belt,” Oct 7, 2026) looked at how fast each state has cleared the pressure from September to December in past years and concluded that, on current production expectations, a storage crunch is unlikely in 2026. It can still pinch in some places, because bins, stocks and crops are spread unevenly. Read their study.

Show the math and sources

Grain vs bins = (September 1, 2026 stocks of corn, soybeans, wheat, sorghum, barley and oats, on- and off-farm) + (2026 corn, soybean and sorghum crop, latest NASS estimate), divided by on-farm plus off-farm storage capacity on December 1, 2025, the latest NASS count. Wheat, barley and oats are harvested by September 1, so they count once, in the stocks. A state gets a ratio only when NASS publishes a state figure for every crop that was at least 2% of its grain last year. Figures are rounded to the million bushels, so they may not add exactly.

Same measure as farmdoc daily (Dhakal and Janzen, “Enough Room for the Harvest? Grain Storage Pressure in the Corn Belt,” Oct 7, 2026). Their article came out before NASS’s October crop report and used the September forecast; this table uses the newer figures, so the order of the states can differ a little from theirs.

Past falls: pressure in each year uses that year’s final crop and that December’s capacity; December 1 use = December 1 stocks of the same six grains over the same capacity. Ranges are the 10th to 90th percentile. The usual-pace line subtracts the state’s average drop from this fall’s pressure; it is a rule of thumb, tested by leaving each past year out in turn.

Source: USDA NASS Quick Stats, Grain Stocks and Crop Production, pulled Oct 16, 2026. Built by scripts/fetch_storage.py; the data: storage.json, history.json. Reviewed by Sigurd Lindquist, CCA.

What these numbers cover. The state numbers are a sample: the elevators the AGSIST network reads, not every elevator. The USDA rows are regional: USDA tracks named markets and origins, not individual elevators, so they explain the regional move your bid rides on. They can’t tell you your own elevator’s spread. Basis is in $/bu against futures. A negative number is normal in most regions most of the time; what matters is the gap from normal.
Questions
Why is my basis so wide right now?

Check three things on this page: your region against its normal, the barge bars, and the storage table. When freight runs above normal, interior bids drop to pay the trip to the export market. When there is more grain than bin space, elevators bid less to slow it down. Local demand, like a plant nearby or a shuttle loader bidding, moves the rest.

Is my elevator ripping me off?

Compare your bid’s basis to your region’s number above. If your elevator is a nickel behind the region, that’s a local conversation. If the whole region is a dollar under its normal, as happens in high-freight years, no elevator in the county could bid it away.

When does basis usually strengthen?

Each region has its own seasonal pattern, so this page compares each region to its own same-week history instead of one national rule. Basis often firms once harvest traffic clears and when processors need grain, but check your own region’s pattern.

Where do these numbers come from?

The state numbers are the elevators the AGSIST network reads. The regional basis, barge rates and bid spreads are USDA AgTransport open data (weekly basis since 2007), refreshed here every Friday. The storage table is USDA NASS. Sources are linked below.
