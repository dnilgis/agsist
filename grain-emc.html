Sigurd Lindquist, Certified Crop Adviser (CCA), Chetek, Wisconsin

    Cooling grain for storage

    To cool, run fans when outside air is 10 to 15 F cooler than the grain (NDSU Extension), and not on hours that wet it. For winter, NDSU says cool grain to 20 to 30 F; Minnesota says 20 to 30 F in the north of the state and 25 to 35 F in the south; Iowa State says 30 to 40 F; Nebraska says near or under 30 F in northern states and 40 F in southern ones.

    Fan hours for one cooling front: 15 divided by cfm per bushel, the rule of thumb Cornell Cooperative Extension gives. Doubling the airflow halves the time. A front is not done until it has come out the top of the grain, so check the top before you shut off.

    How the numbers are worked

    Corn: Modified Chung-Pfost equation with the shelled corn constants in ASABE Standard D245.6, Moisture Relationships of Plant-based Agricultural Products (2007), from Chen and Morey (1989). USDA-ARS uses the same constants for corn storage monitoring.

    RH = exp( -A / (T + C) x exp(-B x M) ) A = 374.34 B = 0.18662 C = 31.696

    Wheat: Modified Henderson equation with the ASAE D245.5 wheat constants. They reproduce the University of Minnesota Extension wheat table at 60 F (7.9, 11.0, 13.7 and 16.9% at 20, 40, 60 and 80% humidity) to within a tenth of a point.

    1 - RH = exp( -K x (T + C) x M^N ) K = 0.000023007 N = 2.2857 C = 55.815

    RH is relative humidity as a decimal, T is air temperature in °C, M is grain moisture in percent dry basis. The page shows wet basis, the number your moisture tester reads: wet = 100 x M / (100 + M).

    Fan heat: warming air does not add water, so its vapor pressure stays put and

    RH at fan = RH outside x es(T outside) / es(T fan) es(T) = 6.1094 x exp( 17.625 x T / (T + 243.04) ) hPa, T in °C

    Saturation vapor pressure from Alduchov and Eskridge (1996), Journal of Applied Meteorology 35:601. Example: 50 F at 80%, warmed 5 F, comes in at 55 F and 67%.

    Limits: I show numbers for air from 20 to 100 F and 20 to 90% humidity. Over 90% the page says the EMC is "over" the 90% value, which already wets any grain at a normal target; under 20% it says "under". Outside 20 to 100 F it shows nothing.

    How sure: published tables for the same corn air do not agree. Against this page:

    Corn, airThis pageMinnesotaKentuckyArkansas

    50 F, 60%13.3%13.8%13.8%no row

    The extension tables run wetter than the ASABE corn equation. So an hour counts as drying only when its EMC is at least 1 point under your target, and anything closer is a close call. "No row" means that table does not list that air. Sources: University of Minnesota Extension, Managing stored grain with aeration and Natural-air corn drying; University of Kentucky, Equilibrium moisture content for grains; University of Arkansas, FSA1074 and the Arkansas Corn Production Handbook.

    Weather: Open-Meteo hourly forecast of 2 m temperature and relative humidity for your point, fetched when you open the page. It is a forecast, so check a gauge before you commit a night of fan time.

    Questions

    What is grain equilibrium moisture content (EMC)?

    It is the moisture grain ends up at if it sits in the same air long enough. Air at 50 F and 60% humidity brings corn to about 13.3% by the ASABE equation this page uses. If that is under the moisture you want, running fans on that air dries the grain. If it is over, the fans put water back in.

    When should I run aeration fans?

    To dry, run them when the air's EMC is under your target moisture. On this page those are the green hours. To cool, run them when outside air is 10 to 15 F cooler than the grain, which is NDSU Extension's rule, and skip hours that would wet it. For winter, NDSU says to cool grain to 20 to 30 F; Minnesota says 20 to 30 F in the north and 25 to 35 F in the south; Iowa State says 30 to 40 F and warns that going much under 30 F adds little and can form ice in the grain.

    What humidity is too high to run fans on corn?

    It depends on the temperature. For corn with a 15.5% target, air dries it to 14.5% or lower while humidity stays under about 64% at 40 F, 68% at 50 F and 71% at 60 F. Above about 71% at 40 F, 74% at 50 F and 77% at 60 F, the air pushes corn over 15.5%. The table on this page gives the limit at every 10 degrees for your own target.

    Does fan heat help dry grain?

    Some. The fan warms the air on its way into the bin, and warmer air with the same water in it has a lower relative humidity. I do not know your fan, so the page starts at 0 F. If you know your plenum runs a few degrees over outside air, type that in and the page works out the new humidity: RH at the fan equals RH outside times the saturation vapor pressure at the outside temperature, divided by the saturation vapor pressure at the fan temperature.

    How accurate is the EMC number?

    The equations are published ASABE fits, but grain varies by hybrid and history, and published tables for the same air do not agree. For corn at 60 F and 70% humidity this page says 14.2%, Minnesota Extension's table says 14.5% and Arkansas Extension's says 15.7%. That is why I only call an hour a drying hour when the EMC is at least 1 point under your target.

    Why are soybeans not on the calculator?

    I could not read the soybean constants in the ASABE standard at a source I trust, and a guessed constant would give you a confident wrong answer. Corn and wheat are here because I checked their equations against the standard and against extension tables. Soybeans go on when I can check theirs.

    Which wheat does the wheat answer fit?

    The constants are the ASAE wheat set, and they match the University of Minnesota Extension wheat table to within a tenth of a point. Soft red winter wheat runs drier: Arkansas Extension lists 13.9% at 60 F and 70% humidity, where this equation says 15.2%. If you grow soft red, the page will call some hours wetting that would actually dry your wheat a little.
