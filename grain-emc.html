When to run aeration fans: will they dry your grain or wet it?

Idea by Zach A., a farmer who emailed and asked for it. Thanks, Zach.

Fan heat is typically 2 to 5 F (NDSU; Minnesota says axial fans add at least 2 to 3 F): read the plenum and the outside air once and enter the difference.

"Holds" is the humidity where grain neither gains nor loses on the wetter of the published fits. "Dries toward" keeps a full point of margin on the main fit and stays under target on the other. The baked table is corn at 15.5%; change the grain or target above and it follows.

Airflow and the cooling front

Fan hours for one cooling front: about 15 divided by cfm per bushel (NDSU, Iowa State and Nebraska Extension); at 0.1 cfm per bushel that is about 150 hours, within the 100 to 200 hours Minnesota Extension gives (Purdue says about 160, Oklahoma State 120 for clean grain). A front is not done until it comes out the top of the grain, so check the top before you shut off. Aeration fans at 0.1 to 0.2 cfm per bushel cool grain; they do not dry it safely. Drying corn takes about 1 cfm per bushel or more.

How the numbers are worked

Corn is shown as a range from two published fits. The low one is the Modified Chung-Pfost equation with the shelled corn constants credited to ASABE Standard D245.6, Moisture Relationships of Plant-based Agricultural Products (2007), from Chen and Morey (1989); USDA-ARS uses the same constants for corn storage monitoring.

RH = exp( -A / (T + C) x exp(-B x M) ) A = 374.34 B = 0.18662 C = 31.696

The high one is the classic Henderson corn fit, which reproduces the University of Kentucky corn table within 0.3 point from 40 to 70% humidity and within 0.6 point from 30 to 90%, at 35 to 90 F.

1 - RH = exp( -K x (T + C) x M^N ) K = 0.000086541 N = 1.8634 C = 49.81

An hour "dries to target" only when the low fit is a full point under your target and the high fit is under it too. Any "wets" or "over target" call uses the high fit.

Wheat: Modified Henderson equation with the ASAE D245.5 wheat constants. They reproduce the University of Minnesota Extension wheat table at 60 F (12.3, 13.7, 15.2 and 16.9% at 50, 60, 70 and 80% humidity, Table 4 of Managing stored grain with aeration) to within a tenth of a point.

1 - RH = exp( -K x (T + C) x M^N ) K = 0.000023007 N = 2.2857 C = 55.815

Soybeans are shown as a range from two fits, like corn, because the published soybean tables split into two camps. The low one is the Modified Henderson equation with the soybean constants long carried in the ASAE D245 standard. It reproduces University of Minnesota Extension's soybean row at 60 F (8.6, 10.5, 12.8 and 15.7% at 50, 60, 70 and 80% humidity, Table 4 of Managing stored grain with aeration) and two soybean numbers from NDSU engineer Ken Hellevang (10.2% at 70 F and 60% on a 2015 slide; about 13.5% at 42 F and 70% in a Jan 2020 release) to within a tenth of a point. It does not match NDSU's own 2011 soybean table; see How sure below.

1 - RH = exp( -K x (T + C) x M^N ) K = 0.000305327 N = 1.2164 C = 134.136

The high one is the Modified Halsey equation, the form the University of Kentucky names on its soybean table ("ASAE Data D245.5 / modified-Halsey equation"). I could not read the standard itself, so I worked the constants back from the printed tables: these three reproduce every soybean value in Kentucky's Table 2 (70 values, 10 to 90% humidity, 35 to 90 F) and Arkansas FSA1074 Table B (196 values, 25 to 90% humidity, 35 to 100 F), 266 of 266, to the tenth of a point printed.

RH = exp( -exp(A + B x T) / M^C ) A = 2.87 B = -0.0054 C = 1.38

The two soybean fits sit up to about a point apart from 40 to 70% humidity and 2 to 8 points apart in very damp air (80 to 90%), where the Halsey tables climb fast. Calls work the same as corn: "dries to target" needs the low fit a full point under your target and the high fit under it too, and any "wets" call uses the high fit. NDSU's 2011 table runs up to 0.5 point wetter than the high fit in dry air (50 to 60% humidity), and the full-point margin covers that: checked against all 30 of its values, no hour this page calls "dries to target" would leave beans over that target by NDSU's numbers. The default soybean target is 13%, Minnesota Extension's number for aerated soybeans kept until June (12% for a year, 11% for longer; Arkansas says 11% for a year).

RH is relative humidity as a decimal, T is air temperature in °C, M is grain moisture in percent dry basis. The page shows wet basis, the number your moisture tester reads: wet = 100 x M / (100 + M). Grain drying down settles a little wetter than grain taking on water in the same air (hysteresis), one more reason published tables differ.

Fan heat: warming air does not add water, so its vapor pressure stays put and

RH at fan = RH outside x es(T outside) / es(T fan) es(T) = 6.1094 x exp( 17.625 x T / (T + 243.04) ) hPa, T in °C

Saturation vapor pressure from Alduchov and Eskridge (1996), Journal of Applied Meteorology 35:601. Example: 50 F at 80%, warmed 5 F, comes in at 55 F and 67%.

Limits: I show numbers for air from 20 to 100 F and 20 to 90% humidity. Over 90% the page shows the 90% value as a floor ("over"), and still calls the hour wet when the air at its real humidity would push grain past your number; under 20% it says "under". Outside 20 to 100 F it shows no number. The corn tables I checked cover 20 to 70 F (Minnesota), 35 to 90 F (Kentucky) and 35 to 100 F (Arkansas); the soybean tables cover 35 to 90 F (Kentucky) and 35 to 100 F (Arkansas), and Minnesota's soybean row is 60 F only.

Questions

What is grain equilibrium moisture content (EMC)?

It is the moisture grain ends up at if it sits in the same air long enough. Air at 50 F and 60% humidity brings corn to about 13.3 to 13.9% and soybeans to about 10.8 to 11.1%, by the two published equations this page uses for each. If that is under the moisture you want, running fans on that air dries the grain. If it is over, the fans put water back in.

When should I run aeration fans?

It depends on the job. For natural-air drying of wet corn, Minnesota Extension says to leave the fan running while corn in the bin is wetter than about 16% and warmer than about 40 F, and NDSU says to turn fans off during extended rain, fog or snow. For cooling, Minnesota says the grain is cooling when the 24-hour average outside is 10 F or more under it; Iowa State says 10 to 20 F. For dry grain in storage, run them on hours when the air's EMC is not over the moisture the grain is stored at. For winter, NDSU says to cool corn to 20 to 25 F; Minnesota says 20 to 30 F in the north of the state and 25 to 35 F in the south; Iowa State says 30 to 40 F and warns that going below 30 F adds little and can form ice in the grain.

What humidity is too high to run fans on corn?

It depends on the temperature and on which published corn equation you trust. Corn held at 15.5% stays there in air of about 65 to 71% humidity at 40 F, 69 to 74% at 50 F and 72 to 77% at 60 F; wetter air than that pushes it over 15.5%. To dry corn toward a 15.5% target with a safety margin, keep the outside humidity under about 64% at 40 F, 68% at 50 F and 71% at 60 F.

Should I shut drying fans off when it is humid or at night?

Not for natural-air drying. Minnesota Extension says that if the fan runs only in the driest weather, the corn ends up badly overdried, and NDSU calls running fans only at night a myth. The exceptions NDSU gives are extended rain, fog and snow. Minnesota says natural-air drying works best at 40 to 60 F and 55 to 75% humidity, and that a heater, if you add one, should warm the air no more than about 5 F.

Does fan heat help dry grain?

Some. Fans warm the air typically 2 to 5 F, by NDSU engineer Ken Hellevang's figure, and Minnesota Extension says axial-flow fans add at least 2 to 3 F. Warmer air with the same water in it has a lower relative humidity: in Minnesota's example, air at 45 F and 70% humidity warmed 5 F comes in at 50 F and 59%. The math is RH at the fan equals RH outside times the saturation vapor pressure at the outside temperature, divided by the saturation vapor pressure at the fan temperature.

How much airflow does it take to dry grain?

Aeration fans at 0.1 to 0.2 cfm per bushel cool grain but do not dry it safely; the top can spoil before a drying front gets there. Drying corn with natural air takes about 1 cfm per bushel or more. For cooling, NDSU, Iowa State and Nebraska Extension give about 15 divided by the cfm per bushel as the fan hours for one cooling front: at 0.1 cfm per bushel that is about 150 hours, within the 100 to 200 hours Minnesota Extension gives.

How accurate is the EMC number?

Published tables for the same air do not agree, so the page shows corn and soybeans as a range from two published fits. For corn at 60 F and 70% humidity the page gives 14.2 to 15.1%; Minnesota Extension's two tables say 14.5% and 14.8%, Kentucky's says 15.0% and Arkansas's says 15.7%. For soybeans in the same air the page gives 12.8 to 13.7%: Kentucky and Arkansas list 13.7% and Minnesota lists 12.8%; NDSU's 2011 table lists 13.0%. An hour counts as drying to target only when the lower fit is a full point under the target and the higher fit is under it too, and any wetting call uses the higher fit.

What humidity is too high to run fans on soybeans?

Soybeans held at 13% stay there in air of about 66 to 68% humidity at 40 F, 67 to 69% at 50 F and 68 to 71% at 60 F, by the two published soybean equations; wetter air than that pushes them over 13%. To dry soybeans toward 13% with a safety margin, keep the outside humidity under about 63% at 40 F, 65% at 50 F and 66% at 60 F. Soybean EMC climbs fast in damp air: at 60 F and 80% humidity beans head for 15.7 to 18.3%.

What moisture should soybeans be stored at?

Minnesota Extension says aerated soybeans keep until June at 13%, for a year at 12% and longer at 11%. Arkansas Extension says 11% for a year, 11% for 30 to 60 days in warm weather and 14% for 30 to 60 days in cool weather. Nebraska CropWatch (Sep 2018, by NDSU grain specialist Ken Hellevang) says 13% is fine in cool weather and advises 11% for beans kept into spring and summer. The soybean target on this calculator starts at 13%, and you can change it.

Which wheat does the wheat answer fit?

The constants are the ASAE wheat set, and they match the University of Minnesota Extension wheat table at 60 F to within a tenth of a point. Soft red winter wheat runs drier: Kentucky's soft red winter table lists 13.7% at 60 F and 70% humidity, where this equation says 15.2%. If you grow soft red, the page will call some hours wetting that would actually dry your wheat a little.

How long will 15% corn keep in the bin?

Minnesota Extension says about 16 months at 50 F, but 5.2 months at 70 F. Dried to 14%, it keeps 11 months at 70 F.
