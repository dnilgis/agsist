Pro Farmer Crop Tour 2026

Each August, scouts on the Pro Farmer Crop Tour drive set routes through seven Midwest states, sample corn and soybean fields, and publish their own estimate of the national yield in bushels per acre. This page checks that estimate against USDA and against how close the tour has come in past years.

Tour figures are Pro Farmer’s; USDA figures from WASDE and Crop Production. The comparison and the math are AGSIST’s, which is not connected to Pro Farmer.

How close the tour has come

Every tour since 2015, against the crop that actually came in. Bar shows how far the tour's final corn number landed from USDA's final yield: left of the line means the tour called it too small, right means too big.

4.1bu
Average tour miss vs the final crop

Over the last 11 tours (2015–2025), Pro Farmer's final corn number missed USDA's eventual final by 4.1 bushels on average. USDA's own August forecast missed by 3.1. USDA came closer in 7 of those 11 years.

The tour has also leaned one way: it came in under the final yield in 7 of 11 years, and in those years it finished 5.3 bushels low on average. Across all 11, the net lean is 2.6 bushels low. It still ran high in 3 of them, the widest by 5.5 bushels, and it landed exactly on the final once.

How to read the tour's number against its record

Set the number above against what the tour's own record says to expect of it. Across 11 tours it has finished below the crop that actually came in 7 times and above it 3, landing on it exactly once. 7 of those 11 misses fall between -5.2 and +0.9 bushels, the widest between -9.5 and +5.5, and it has landed within 6 bushels of the final crop in 10 of 11 years. That is a count of past years, not a forecast, and not a reason to adjust the tour's figure: adding its average lean back on tested worse than leaving it alone.

Nightly results

Scouts run two routes at once, Monday through Thursday, and states report at the nightly meetings. Corn is a sampled yield in bushels per acre. Soybeans are pod counts in a three-foot square. More pods means more beans, but pods do not convert cleanly into bushels, so treat them as a direction, not a yield.

Do pod counts predict soybean yield?

AGSIST working paper · 19 August 2026, 2026 line added 16 October · 28 state-years

Nobody quotes a pod count when they talk soybean yield. The reason usually given is that pod counts don’t convert, and that is half true. They do not convert through any published formula. But they carry real signal, and it can be measured. We could not find anyone who had published that measurement, so here it is.

Want a yield for your own field? The soybean yield calculator works it from plants, pods per plant, seeds per pod and seed size.

What the tour actually does with soybeans

Pro Farmer’s scouts measure corn as a yield in the field: ear counts, grain length, kernel rows. They never measure a soybean yield at all. The published method is to count pods: “Count all pods on the three plants, any pod ¼″ or larger, and average.” That count is normalised to a three-foot square and published per state. No state soybean yield is published, ever.

Their stated reason is exact, and it is the reason this page has to be careful: “The number of pods it takes to make a bushel is different in each state and varies from year to year.”

The measurement

Twenty-eight state-years: seven tour states, 2022 through 2025. Pod counts as Pro Farmer published them. Soybean yields as USDA NASS finally published them, in the Crop Production Annual Summary each January, cross-checked against the state field-office releases.

Each dot is one state in one year, shown as a difference from that state’s own four-year average. Pooling every state together gives r = +0.85, but most of that is just South Dakota having both low pod counts and low yields. It measures the gap between states, not the thing anyone wants to know. Subtracting each state’s own average isolates the real question: when a state’s pod count moves, does its yield move with it?

The corn side of the same tour, run through exactly the same arithmetic, is the control. Shaded band is 95%.

The misses above are in-sample. Each state’s mean, which the within-state fit subtracts, includes the year being predicted, so a call made ahead of the count would miss by more than 2.89 bu. Both within-state misses are divided by 20 degrees of freedom, not 26: the seven state means take the place of the one intercept, so they spend six more.

The answer. Pod counts are about 1.8× looser than the tour’s corn reading, a typical miss of 5.3% of mean yield against 3.0%, in-sample. That is worse, and it is nothing like useless. The tour measures corn directly and never measures a soybean yield at all, so a factor of 1.8 is a small price for a number that is only ever counted, never weighed. Pod counts deserve to be quoted. They deserve to be quoted with a band around them.

The divisor everyone repeats is wrong

The rule of thumb you will hear is “divide the pod count by 27.” It traces to a single 2013 DTN blog post quoting an unnamed source, and the journalist who printed it attached her own warning: “I just wouldn’t put too much stock in that yield estimate’s accuracy.” It is not a Pro Farmer number and never was.

Our twenty-eight state-years put it at 22.0 as a simple ratio of pods to bushels, and 23.5 as the slope of the pooled fit, which mixes differences between states with changes within a state. To turn one state’s change in pods into bushels, use the within-state fit from the table above instead: about 44 pods to the bushel (0.0227 bu per pod). The 22.0 and 23.5 sit inside the 21.8–25.8 range implied by Pro Farmer’s own published national pairs. Three separate routes land in the same place, and it is not 27.

Worth knowing if you are tempted by a textbook: the extension formulas (Purdue, Ohio State, Iowa State, and USDA’s own objective-yield conversion) give 15 to 18 on this basis at 3,000–3,600 seeds a pound; with larger seed (2,500 a pound) the same arithmetic gives about 12. Divide a Pro Farmer count by 15 and you will overstate yield by 47 to 57% (22.0 and 23.5 against 15); at 18 the overstatement is 22 to 31%. The reason is that Pro Farmer counts any pod a quarter-inch or larger off three plants on fields chosen by the route. Different scale, on purpose. Never import those constants.

What would break this

Four years is four years. Leaving any one state out moves the correlation between +0.60 and +0.74, which is stable. Leaving out a year is the worry: drop 2025 and it falls to +0.47. One season is carrying a lot of the signal, which is exactly what four points per state should be expected to do. More years would help, and the data exists: every Pro Farmer nightly report carries a year-ago comparison, so each report read back adds two years.

Pod weight compensates, and the tour cannot see it. USDA’s own eleven-state pod counts fell 12.6% from 2018 to 2019 while yield fell only 6.3%, because the average pod got heavier. Seed size alone swings a yield estimate by about 17% with no change in pod count whatsoever. The tour counts pods and never weighs them, so that whole term is invisible to it. This is the structural ceiling, and no amount of extra data removes it.

One state, one year, is a coin flip and a half. On year-over-year change, pod count and yield move the same direction 14 times in 21. The corn control manages 16 of 21. Read a single state’s pod count as a yield signal and you will be right about two times in three.

What we will not do

Print a single number and call it a soybean yield. On this fit the 95% band is roughly ±6 bushels an acre, and pooled it is a little over ±8. A point estimate inside a band that wide tells you very little. If we ever publish an estimate it will be a range, it will carry the band, and it will be labelled as ours: a calibration, not a translation of anything Pro Farmer said.

Where the numbers came from

Pod counts: Pro Farmer Crop Tour state results, 2022–2025, as published each night of the tour. Soybean yields: USDA NASS Crop Production Annual Summary, the January-following edition each year, cross-checked against NASS state field-office releases and Executive Briefings. Every cell sourced; none interpolated or filled in. Two cautions: Ohio and South Dakota 2025 come from the NASS State Agriculture Overview rather than a January field-office release, and NASS revised several 2021 state figures upward between the January 2022 and January 2023 prints, so 2021 is not used in any fit on this page. Arithmetic is ours; the underlying figures are not.

How to use this. The tour is the only wide, boots-in-the-field count of this crop, and in a year where the trade doubts USDA it moves the board. But it samples fields along fixed routes in seven states, which is not the same thing as measuring the country, and the record above says it has not been more accurate than USDA's August number. Read the nightly state figures for where the crop is better or worse than expected, and be careful about trading the national estimate as though it settles the argument.

What counts as the final. Final yield here means the figure USDA published the following January in its annual summary. USDA revises a few of those later by a tenth to half a bushel; this page uses the January number for every year so the comparison stays consistent.

Our own number is on the record too. Before every WASDE we lock the nowcast and a call on which way USDA will move, then grade it after. In August, before the tour rolled, we called corn and beans both revising up. USDA revised both down, and both count as misses. Every call since is on the record.

Common questions

How accurate is the Pro Farmer Crop Tour?
Over the 11 tours from 2015 through 2025, Pro Farmer's final national corn yield estimate missed USDA's eventual final figure by 4.1 bushels per acre on average. USDA's own August forecast missed by 3.1 bushels over the same years, and USDA came closer in 7 of the 11. The tour has also leaned one way: it came in under the final yield in 7 of 11 years, and in those years it finished 5.3 bushels low on average. Across all 11, the net lean is 2.6 bushels low. It still ran high in 3 of them, the widest by 5.5 bushels, and it landed exactly on the final once.
What do the tour's soybean pod counts mean?
Scouts count soybean pods inside a three-foot by three-foot square. More pods generally points to a bigger bean crop, but pod counts do not convert cleanly into bushels per acre, and the tour does not publish a state soybean yield. Its only soybean bushel figures are Pro Farmer's national yield and production estimates, released at the end of the week. The pod counts section on this page measures what the counts have predicted against USDA's final state yields since 2022.
When does the Pro Farmer Crop Tour release its results?
Scouts run two routes Monday through Thursday, and state results are announced at nightly meetings during the tour week. The national corn yield and soybean production estimates come out at the end of the week, after all samples are in.
